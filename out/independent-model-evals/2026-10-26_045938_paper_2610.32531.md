*The authors build NTBench, a prompt-injection benchmark whose attacks aim at responses that resemble a legitimate answer but contain an incorrect or harmful change, and test seven LLMs on it [1][2].*

### Hypothesis

The authors ask whether resistance to the fixed-target prompt-injection attacks they test implies resistance to closely related incorrect or harmful outcomes, suggesting it may not because systems could reject attacks that obviously abandon the user's request while accepting small damaging changes [1][2].

### Method

The authors construct NTBench from legitimate tasks in existing datasets [1][2]. For each task, a legitimate reference outcome guides an LLM to generate target-instruction pairs in two tracks, one targeting incorrect outcomes and one targeting harmful outcomes in responses or agent tool calls, with harmful targets sometimes accompanying otherwise correct task completion [1][2]. LLM-based checks plus human validation confirm the intended deviations, and success is judged by response judgments or tool-call checks, excluding deviations already observed without injection [1][2]. They evaluate seven LLMs on language tasks from ten datasets and on agentic tasks from AgentDojo and AgentDyn, using a common budget of three attack executions per sample and comparing against a fixed-target baseline [1][2].

### Findings

- The authors report that both near-target tracks achieve higher overall attack success rates than the fixed-target baseline for every evaluated model in each setting [1][2].
- On language tasks, the authors report increases of 9.3 to 35.8 percentage points for incorrect targets and 1.7 to 17.3 percentage points for harmful targets over the baseline [1][2].
- The authors report that Meta-SecAlign-70B, trained to resist prompt injection, rises from 3.2% ASR@3 on the baseline to 36.7% for incorrect targets and 18.4% for harmful targets [2].
- The authors report that the two tracks yield different model rankings, so the choice of tested outcomes affects both the vulnerabilities revealed and the relative robustness attributed to systems [2].
- The authors report that resistance to the evaluated fixed-target attacks does not imply resistance to related incorrect or harmful outcomes, and that this holds without model-specific adaptive search or a larger attack-execution budget [1][2].

### Limitations

- (authors) Generation of targets is explicitly assisted by benchmark references: the generator receives the user request, original context, an accepted reference outcome, validation criteria, and for agents the tool descriptions and task-relevant initial state [2].
- (authors) The reported rates describe vulnerability for the selected tasks, the attacks generated for them, and the number of attempts allowed [2].
- (authors) The attacker model allows only addition of instructions at designated locations in untrusted content, with no editing, deletion, replacement, or reordering of original content, and no direct access to tool permissions [2].
- (authors) The work is a preprint with no journal reference and no author comment provided [1].
- (our reading) Results cover seven LLMs, ten language datasets, and two agent benchmarks; generalization to other models, tasks, and systems is not shown.

### Possible applications

- (our reading, speculative; not tested by the paper) Software teams building LLM or agent features that consume retrieved documents, external messages, or tool outputs could evaluate them for small, task-plausible errors or harmful edits rather than only obvious hijacking.
- (our reading, speculative; not tested by the paper) Teams could add near-target test cases, anchored on a correct reference output, to their own pre-release robustness checks.
- (our reading, speculative; not tested by the paper) A pass on fixed-target injection benchmarks should not be used alone as evidence that a system will not produce plausible-looking but incorrect or unsafe outputs.

### Terms

- **prompt injection**: Embedding malicious instructions in untrusted external content so that an LLM or agent departs from the user's request or system instructions [2].
- **near-target prompt injection**: An attack whose desired response resembles a legitimate answer but introduces an incorrect or harmful change [1][2].
- **NTBench**: The Near-Target Benchmark introduced by the authors, built on legitimate tasks from existing datasets with attacks targeting incorrect or harmful deviations [1][2].
- **fixed-target baseline**: The comparison baseline of attacks aimed at a fixed, often obvious target that departs from the user's task, against which near-target attacks are measured [1][2].
- **ASR@3**: Attack success rate measured with a common budget of three attack executions per sample [2].
- **reference outcome**: A legitimate accepted answer used as the anchor for generating nearby incorrect or harmful targets [1][2].
- **AgentDojo and AgentDyn**: The two agent benchmarks used for the agentic-task setting [1][2].
