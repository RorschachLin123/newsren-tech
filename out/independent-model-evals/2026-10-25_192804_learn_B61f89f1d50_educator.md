### Learning goals

- Explain what a benchmark is and why a shared set of questions allows comparison between models.
- Interpret an error bar (e.g., 0.932 ± 0.016) as a range of plausible values, not an exact score.
- Distinguish an independent evaluation from a developer's self-reported numbers, and explain why the difference matters.
- Recognize that one source reporting a result is weaker evidence than two separate sources agreeing, and that even agreement may reflect different methods.
- Describe how a benchmark's difficulty tiers can produce very different scores for the same model.

### Activity

Error-Bar Detective (about 12 minutes of the 20). Step 1 (2 min): Write two lines on the board: 'DeepSeek model, GPQA diamond: 0.932 ± 0.016' [1] and 'Muse Spark model, GPQA diamond: 0.886 ± 0.019' [2]. Step 2 (3 min): In pairs, students compute each range (low and high ends) and sketch both as bars on a number line. Step 3 (3 min): Pairs decide whether the bars overlap and write one sentence on what that implies, noting that the tests were run on different dates [1][2]. ### Misconceptions to watch

- 'A higher score means the model is simply better.' A score reflects performance on one particular test, and error bars and test dates both affect how far a comparison can be trusted [1][2].
- '0.932 is an exact figure.' It is a mean with a stated uncertainty of ± 0.016 [1].
- 'Independent means infallible.' Independent testers can still differ in methodology, and the Artificial Analysis figure is undated in the material provided and may be measured differently [3].
- 'Two sources agreeing means the result is certain.' Agreement is encouraging, as with Epoch's 0.886 and Artificial Analysis's 89.0% for Muse Spark [2][3], but it does not remove differences in method.
- 'A benchmark measures general intelligence.' A benchmark measures performance on its own specific questions, and the same model can score very differently across tiers [1][2].
